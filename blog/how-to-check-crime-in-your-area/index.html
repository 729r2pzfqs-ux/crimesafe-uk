How to Check Crime in Your Area — Free Tools & Tips

Whether you're moving to a new area, curious about your neighbourhood, or just want to stay informed, checking local crime data is easier than you might think. Here's a complete guide to the free tools available.

Quick Start: Enter your postcode at crimesafe.uk/postcode to see all crimes reported near you in the last month. It takes 10 seconds and you don't need to create an account.

Why Check Crime Data?

There are plenty of good reasons to look at local crime statistics:

  • Buying or renting a home — crime rates directly affect property values and quality of life. Understanding the safety profile of an area helps you make an informed decision.
  • Personal safety — knowing which types of crime occur near you helps you take sensible precautions.
  • Community awareness — staying informed about local issues means you can report suspicious activity and support neighbourhood initiatives.
  • Insurance considerations — home and car insurance premiums are partly based on local crime rates. Understanding your area's profile can help when shopping for cover.

Step 1: Search by Postcode

The fastest way to check crime near a specific address is to use a postcode search. CrimeSafe UK's postcode checker pulls data directly from the police API and shows you:

  • All crimes reported within a one-mile radius in the latest month
  • A breakdown by crime type (violence, theft, burglary, ASB, drugs, etc.)
  • The outcome of each crime (investigation complete, unable to prosecute, etc.)
  • A map showing where each incident was reported

Simply visit crimesafe.uk/postcode, type in a full UK postcode (e.g. SW1A 1AA), and hit search. Results appear in seconds.

Tip: If you're house-hunting, bookmark the postcode pages for properties you're considering. Come back each month to see the latest data and spot any trends.

Step 2: Use the Interactive Crime Map

Maps make it much easier to spot patterns. Our street crime map lets you:

  • Zoom into any street in England and Wales
  • See crime markers clustered on the map
  • Click any marker to see what type of crime was reported and when
  • Toggle between crime types to filter the view

The map is particularly useful for visualising whether crime is spread evenly across an area or concentrated in specific spots. You might find that a neighbourhood has a higher overall rate, but it's all clustered around a single high street or transport hub — while the residential streets are quiet.

Step 3: Search by Street Name

Want even more precision? Our street crime search lets you look up specific streets and roads. This is ideal when you have a particular address in mind — say, the street where you're considering buying a flat.

Street-level data shows you exactly what has been reported on or near that road, giving you the most granular view possible. Bear in mind that police data is "snapped" to nearby points for privacy, so a crime shown on your street may have actually occurred a few doors along.

Step 4: Compare Areas

Raw numbers don't mean much without context. A postcode with 50 crimes in a month could be perfectly average for a busy city centre or unusually high for a quiet village. That's why comparing areas is essential.

How to Read Crime Data

Crime statistics can be confusing if you're not used to them. Here's what the key terms mean:

Crime Categories Explained

  • Violence and sexual offences — covers a very wide range, from verbal threats and harassment to serious assault. Most "violent" crimes don't involve physical injury.
  • Anti-social behaviour — nuisance behaviour that doesn't meet the threshold for a criminal offence: noise complaints, rowdy groups, littering, minor disputes.
  • Burglary — someone entering a building to steal. Split into residential (homes) and commercial (businesses).
  • Vehicle crime — theft of or from a vehicle, including catalytic converter theft.
  • Shoplifting — theft from a shop. Usually concentrated on high streets and retail parks.
  • Criminal damage and arson — vandalism, graffiti, and deliberate fires.
  • Drugs — possession and supply offences. Higher numbers often reflect proactive policing rather than higher drug use.
  • Other theft — a catch-all for stealing that doesn't fit other categories, including bicycle theft and theft from the person.

Understanding Outcomes

Every crime has an outcome recorded against it. Common outcomes include:

  • Investigation complete, no suspect identified — the police looked into it but couldn't find who did it. This is the most common outcome for crimes like theft and criminal damage.
  • Unable to prosecute suspect — a suspect was identified but the case couldn't proceed (insufficient evidence, witness withdrawal, etc.).
  • Under investigation — the case is still open.
  • Offender given a caution — the offender admitted guilt and received a formal warning rather than being charged.
  • Court result unavailable — the case went to court but the outcome hasn't been recorded in the data yet.

Tips for Home Buyers

If you're buying a property, crime data should be part of your research alongside schools, transport, and amenities. Here's how to use it wisely:

  1. Check multiple months — a single month can be unrepresentative. Look at three to six months of data to get a reliable picture. A one-off incident (like a large-scale event nearby) can spike the numbers temporarily.
  2. Focus on crime types that matter to you — high shoplifting near a high street doesn't necessarily affect residential safety. Look specifically at burglary, violence, and vehicle crime in the streets around the property.
  3. Check adjacent postcodes too — crime doesn't respect postcode boundaries. Search the postcodes either side of the property as well.
  4. Compare with your current area — search your own postcode first so you have a baseline. Is the new area better, worse, or about the same?
  5. Visit at night — data tells you what's been reported, but visiting an area after dark gives you a feel for how it actually feels. Are the streets well-lit? Are there people around? Does it feel comfortable?
  6. Ask the estate agent — they're legally required to disclose known issues. Ask about any problems they're aware of in the immediate area.
  7. Check long-term trends — an area with a slightly higher crime rate but a strong downward trend may be a better investment than one that's safe today but getting worse.

Tips for Renters

Renters have the advantage of flexibility — if an area doesn't work out, you can move on. But it's still worth doing your homework:

  • Check the specific street — rental listings are concentrated on busier roads, which may have different crime profiles to quieter residential streets.
  • Consider ground-floor risk — if you're looking at a ground-floor flat, pay particular attention to burglary rates in the area.
  • Look at vehicle crime — if you have a car and will be parking on the street, check vehicle crime rates. Some areas have significantly higher rates of theft from vehicles.
  • Check ASB levels — anti-social behaviour can be more of a daily nuisance than actual crime. High ASB areas may have noise, litter, or late-night disturbances.

Other Free Resources

Besides CrimeSafe UK, there are other sources of information:

  • Police.uk — the official government crime mapping tool. CrimeSafe UK uses the same underlying data but presents it in a more user-friendly format.
  • Your local police force website — many forces publish ward-level crime summaries and neighbourhood policing updates.
  • ONS Crime Statistics — the Office for National Statistics publishes quarterly bulletins with national and regional analysis.
  • Local council websites — some councils publish their own safety data, community safety partnership reports, and information about local initiatives.

Important Things to Keep in Mind

Crime data is a powerful tool, but it has limitations. Keep these points in mind:

  • Data has a lag — police data is published monthly with a two-month delay. The latest figures you see may not reflect the current situation.
  • Location is approximate — for privacy reasons, crimes are mapped to the nearest point on a street rather than the exact address. A crime shown on your road might have happened a few doors away.
  • Not all crime is reported — the data only covers crimes that were reported to police and formally recorded. Minor thefts, anti-social behaviour, and some fraud often go unreported.
  • Context matters — a high crime rate doesn't automatically mean an area is dangerous. Areas near hospitals record more violence (because assault victims report there), transport hubs record more theft, and areas with proactive policing record more drug offences.
  • Statistics are not destiny — even in higher-crime areas, the vast majority of residents are never victims of crime. Good personal security habits matter more than the postcode you live in.
